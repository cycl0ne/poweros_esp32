# usb/aic8800.device

`DEVS:usb/aic8800.device` is an AICSemi AIC8800 USB Wi-Fi stick as a
station. It answers the network device API with Ethernet framing, and the
wireless requests to scan, join and leave (`sdk/devices/wireless.zig`).
It runs on the ESP32-P4, whose USB ports are high speed; the stick's boot
ROM refuses a slower port.

The stick runs a firmware of AICSemi's that does the 802.11 MAC itself -
it scans, authenticates and associates when it is asked to - and leaves
WPA2's keys to the host. The firmware is fetched once by
`scripts/fetch-aic8800.sh` at a pinned commit and checked against its
sha256; nothing of it is committed, since its licence for passing it on is
not clear. The build puts it on the ESP32-P4's disk in
`DEVS:usb/firmware/aic8800/` - not in `DEVS:usb/` itself, where every
file is looked at as a class driver. A tree without it builds the device,
which then leaves the stick as it found it.

## Two roles, one stick

Plugged in, the stick is its boot ROM, A69C:8800, with one vendor
interface and two bulk endpoints. Every transfer to it is a debug
message: memory read and written, a masked write, a 1 KiB block written,
the program started. The device reads the chip's revision, gives it the
firmware at 0x110000 and the revision's patch and its tables, runs the
patch table - whose Bluetooth mode it sets to Wi-Fi only - writes the
configuration patch, and starts the firmware. The stick leaves the bus and
comes back as A69C:8801: three interfaces, the Wi-Fi's the vendor's own
with an endpoint in, one out for frames and one out for messages. The
other two are Bluetooth's, and not taken.

Everything runs on the device's worker, a DOS process, since the firmware
is read from files: the bind (on the bus's process) only opens the pipes
and hands the worker the stick, and the unbind takes the transfers back
and waits for the worker to let it go.

## The firmware brought up

`bringup.zig`, in order: the registers the firmware needs, the stack
started, its version asked, the transmit power levels and the RF
calibration, the MAC address from the eFuse, the MAC reset, its version,
its capabilities (one stream, HT at 20 and 40 MHz), the channels it may
use (1 to 13, and 36 to 48 at 5 GHz where the stick has the band), the
MAC started, the coexistence with Bluetooth, and a station interface
added, whose number every later message names. The log says the
firmware's version, the bands and the address.

## Messages and the read always out

A message out is a 4-byte USB header (the length, type 0x11), a word of
nothing, the message's header - its id, the task it is for, the host's
task, its parameters' length - and the parameters. An id is its task in
the top six bits and its number below.

A transfer in holds records: a confirmation or an indication, with the
same header; a frame received, behind a 56-byte header of what the radio
saw; the status of a frame sent; a line of the firmware's own log, which
goes to the system's.

While the Wi-Fi runs one bulk read is always out, 8 KiB, which a record
the size of an A-MSDU fits whole. Its records are handed on as they
come. A request to the firmware waits for its confirmation through that
same read: the worker waits for the read or the timer, and whatever else
the transfer holds is handed on as it would be. A record's handler
therefore never sends a request itself; what needs one - a key to
install, a join - is noted, and done when the worker is back in its loop.

## Frames

**Out**: the Ethernet frame without its header, behind the USB header
(type 1) and a 28-byte descriptor: the payload's length, a status word,
both addresses and the type, the queue, the traffic class, the station
interface and the access point's station number. The firmware makes the
802.11 frame and encrypts it. One transfer is out at a time; the
station's own EAPOL frame goes before the unit's writes, and asks for its
status.

**In**: an 802.11 data frame, decrypted, its cipher header left in place.
`frames.zig` reads its addresses by the to/from-DS bits, steps over the
QoS field and the cipher's header, and turns the LLC/SNAP header into an
Ethernet type - each subframe of an A-MSDU on its own. EAPOL frames go to
the key handshake, the rest to the unit.

## Joining

S2_SETOPTIONS is answered at once; the join follows on the worker: a scan
for the network, its strongest access point (or the one named), and the
firmware told to connect. An open network is joined when the firmware
says it has associated, and the unit has its carrier.

A WPA2-Personal network takes `LIBS:wpa.library`
(`sdk/docs/guides/network.md`): the network's key made from the
passphrase, and kept for the next join of the same network and
passphrase; the station's RSN element made from the access point's and
put into the connect request, with the host keeping the port; and a key
handshake made before the firmware is told to connect. The access point's
EAPOL frames go to the handshake, its replies go out with a status asked
for, and the status tells it a frame has gone. After message 4 the keys
come through its hook and are installed, the pairwise key and then the
group key, and the port is opened; then the unit has its carrier. A
group rekey comes the same way.

A network asked for stays wanted until it is left. Off it for any reason
but its own leave - thrown off, refused, not found, not joined in fifteen
seconds - the station tries again after a pause: a second, doubling to a
minute, back to a second once joined. The first join after the board
restarts is often refused, the access point still holding the station
from before; the next is let in. A stick plugged in again joins the
network wanted.

## The interface

The first time a stick comes up the device's own DOS process runs
`C:net/AddNetInterface WLAN1 QUIET NOWAIT`, if `DEVS:NetInterfaces/WLAN1`
is there (it is, on the ESP32-P4's disk). The file says `Start =
DEVICE`, so the boot's `AddNetInterface ALL` leaves it out; a `Network`
line in it has that network joined, its passphrase from
`ENVARC:Sys/net/networks/<network>`. The interface stays from then on,
down while no stick is plugged in.

## Tests

The host tests (`./zig build test`) cover the message framing and the
records of a transfer, the boot ROM's sequence against a pretend chip,
the scan's request and a beacon read, the connect and key requests, and
frames both ways, an A-MSDU among them. wpa.library has its own: the keys
against IEEE 802.11's and RFC 3394's vectors, the elements, every message
of the handshake against a pretend access point, and the library through
its jump table.

## Proved on the board

On the Olimex ESP32-P4-PC: the firmware loaded and started, the Wi-Fi
brought up (version, both bands, the eFuse's address), a scan listing the
networks in range with their security, two WPA2-Personal networks joined
- the 4-way handshake through wpa.library, both keys in, the port open -
an address from DHCP, ping, names resolved, the clock set with
`TimeSync`, pages fetched with `HTTPGet` over HTTP and HTTPS, 1 MB
downloaded; a join refused at boot joined a second later by itself.

## Not done yet

- **Received frames are not put back in order.** The firmware asks the
  host to reorder an A-MPDU's frames; the unit takes them as they come,
  which TCP copes with, at some cost.
- **Fragments are dropped**, and so is a frame of the 4-address format.
- **Throughput** is held by the TCP windows (8 KiB each way): about
  70 KiB/s over a 70 ms internet path.
- **Only WPA2-Personal with CCMP** - no WPA3 (SAE), no 802.1X, no
  TKIP-only network, no network that requires protected management
  frames.
- **S2_GETCRYPTTYPES** is not answered.
- **Another network on the same interface** keeps what the first one's
  routers said over IPv6 - routes, prefixes, name servers - until their
  lifetimes run out; the stack does not yet notice the network changed.
